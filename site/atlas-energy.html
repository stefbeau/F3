Power and climate

AI runs on electricity. Demand from data centres is on course to double within four years, and in the two largest markets most of the new supply comes from gas and coal.

Electricity used by data centres

What is happening

The International Energy Agency expects data centres to use twice as much electricity in 2030 as today, with AI responsible for about three-quarters of the increase.2 The capacity of the large AI sites it tracks rose from under 2 GW to about 17 GW in two years.6

The supply mix differs sharply by region. By 2030 gas is expected to provide about 60% of the power for data centres in the United States, coal about half in China, and renewables about 70% in the European Union.3

Power, not chips, is now the constraint. A data centre can be built in two to three years, while grid connections and new generation take four to eight, so operators are installing their own gas plants.7 Five large technology companies spent more than $400 billion on capital projects in 2025.5

In absolute terms the sector is still small: about 180 million tonnes of CO₂ a year, or 0.5% of emissions from burning fuels. It is one of the few sectors whose emissions are still rising.4

The ledger

What is gained

  • AI can cut emissions elsewhere: finding methane leaks from satellites sooner, and running power plants and grids more efficiently.4

What it costs

  • Emissions from data centres up 70% by 2030.2
  • New gas plants built for data centres will run for decades.
  • Local grids are strained, and connection queues delay other users.

What is not known

  • Whether AI’s savings elsewhere will outweigh its own footprint. No study settles it.
  • How much efficiency gains in chips will offset growth in use.
  • Whether all the announced capacity gets built.

Non-CO2 emissions are prescribed, not modelled (D-021). Scenario warming starts about 0.04 to 0.07 K below observed for the current decade (D-030, Proposed). Cost of the D-021 re-run rule with all 841 members: about 19 s per annual re-run.

What links the two halves of this page, and what does not. The emissions behind this curve are prescribed. They do not respond to the data-centre build-out reported above, so the IEA’s 70% rise in data-centre emissions is not in the F3 curve.

Climate damage to output. With the damage switch on, F3 lowers economic output by a share that grows with warming:

Damage option20502100
Low (Nordhaus 2017)0.9%1.9%
Central (Barrage and Nordhaus 2024)1.2%2.8%
High (Howard and Sterner 2017)3.6%8.1%

These are level-of-output effects. The high option is not the upper end of the literature: growth-effect estimates are larger and are not represented.

Data-centre electricity: withheld. F3 computes it but does not show it. In the recorded runs it leaves any plausible range: about 20,000 TWh in 2030, against the roughly 1,000 TWh the IEA expects. The cause is a growth sector with no physical limit on power. An approved change (decision D-066) will let data centres grow only as fast as powered capacity can be built. It is not built yet.
