Military use

AI already helps armies find targets and steer drones. Spending is rising fast, and talks on binding rules have not produced a treaty.

What is happening

On the battlefield. Testimony to the US Congress in September 2026 describes AI targeting systems in use in Ukraine, Gaza and Iran, and names eight countries with high-end systems: China, Israel, North Korea, Russia, South Korea, Ukraine, the United Kingdom and the United States.10 The UN Secretary-General’s 2025 report lists uses that already exist, including drones that keep steering to a target under electronic jamming.15

Does it protect civilians or endanger them? The evidence is disputed. Supporters argue that faster data processing can make targeting more precise and reduce human error, and a Pentagon spokesperson has said autonomous weapons might pose less risk to civilians than conventional ones.126 Researchers who studied reporting from Gaza conclude that systems built to produce many targets quickly contributed to strikes that increased civilian harm, partly because of accuracy limits.12 Human Rights Watch says adoption is outpacing the testing that lawful use depends on.11

Rules. The UN General Assembly adopted a resolution on lethal autonomous weapons in December 2025 by 164 votes to 6. The United States, which had supported the 2023 and 2024 texts, voted against, with Russia, Israel, Belarus, Burundi and North Korea.2 A second resolution on AI in the military domain passed its committee 166 to 5.1 States held their first UN exchange on military AI in Geneva in June 2026.3 In August 2026 the UN Secretary-General and the president of the Red Cross renewed their call for binding rules, and a review conference opened in Geneva on 16 November.45 The United States has said it is not convinced existing law is insufficient.6

Nuclear weapons. Just over half of the nuclear-armed states have said explicitly that they will keep humans in control of decisions to use nuclear weapons.13 SIPRI warns that military AI can raise the risk of escalation even when it is not built into nuclear systems.13 A UN resolution on AI in nuclear command and control also passed in 2025, with the United States voting no.1

The ledger

What is gained

  • Faster processing of intelligence, and claims of greater precision.12
  • Cheap autonomous systems can defend against larger forces, as Ukraine’s use of drones shows.
  • SIPRI also studies ways AI could reduce nuclear risk, for example in verification.14

What it costs

  • Reported civilian harm where systems generate targets faster than people can check them.12
  • An arms race: one request rising from millions to tens of billions in a year.7
  • Spread to more states and to armed groups.10

What is not known

  • How accurate deployed systems are. Their testing is not public.11
  • Whether autonomy lowers or raises civilian deaths overall. Both are claimed; neither is measured.
  • Whether any binding rule will be agreed, and whether the largest military powers would join it.
