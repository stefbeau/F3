Robots

AI is leaving the screen. Factory robots are at a record, and humanoid robots went from prototypes to tens of thousands shipped in a year. Most of those are not yet doing productive work.

What is happening

Factory robots. The stock of industrial robots passed five million in 2025, and factories installed more than 600,000 in a year for the first time.2 The industry federation forecasts 655,000 installations in 2026 and 806,000 in 2029.1 These are fixed arms and similar machines doing set tasks.

Humanoids. Shipments in the first half of 2026 were three to five times those of a year earlier, depending on who counts.35 Two Chinese makers supply about three in four.6 More than 60% went to entertainment, performance, research and data collection, not production lines.4 One analyst expects that through 2028 fewer than 100 companies will take humanoid trials past the experimental stage.5

Forecasts span two orders of magnitude. One firm sees 500,000 humanoids a year by 2030; another sees 26 million a year by 2040.310 These are market forecasts by firms that sell research, and orders announced often differ from robots deployed.6

Work. A 2026 study by Anthropic estimates that today’s robots could perform three-quarters of physical tasks in the United States, covering 34% of working hours, mostly in controlled settings, and that they are cheaper than people for only 0.3% of tasks.7 It finds that jobs more exposed to robots saw larger falls in wages and employment over fifty years, and that exposed workers are more often male, less educated and lower paid.7 A study of 20 EU countries finds the effect depends on the stage: early adoption tends to add jobs, and the relation changes in sectors already robotised.8 The industry federation’s own review says robots replace tasks more than whole occupations.9

Safety. A fixed robot arm stops when power is cut. A humanoid balances actively and can fall. The first international safety standard for such robots is not finished.5

The ledger

What is gained

  • Dangerous, dirty and repetitive tasks taken over by machines.9
  • Higher productivity where robots are adopted, and in early adoption more jobs.8
  • Help in sectors short of workers.

What it costs

  • Lower wages and employment in the most exposed jobs, on past evidence.7
  • Exposure falls on lower-paid, less educated workers.7
  • Each robot needs metals, batteries, chips and electricity; no total was found for this atlas.
  • New safety risks from machines that walk among people.

What is not known

  • How fast humanoids become useful outside controlled settings.
  • Real numbers: shipments, deployments and prices are estimates that disagree.
  • Whether cost falls enough to matter for most physical work, and when.
  • The material and energy footprint of millions of robots.

F3 and this topic

Not in the model

F3 does not model this. The AI sector represents automation through computing alone. It holds no stock of robots and no metals, batteries or electricity for building and running them.

The figures on this page are context for readers. They do not enter any F3 run.
