Safety, access and rules

Capabilities are advancing faster than the means to control them, access to the technology is very uneven, and the first binding rules are arriving in stages.

What the scientific assessment says

The International AI Safety Report 2026, chaired by Yoshua Bengio, sorts the risks of general-purpose AI into three groups: malicious use, malfunctions, and systemic risks.2 It reports growing real-world evidence for several of them, including deepfakes, help with cyberattacks and concerns about biological misuse.4

It finds that capabilities advanced quickly over the past year, with leading systems reaching gold-medal level on International Mathematical Olympiad questions, and that existing safety practices are not keeping pace.2 It also warns that strong results in controlled tests do not always carry over to the real world.3 The report makes no policy recommendations.5

Who has access

Only 32 countries host data centres specialised for AI.8 Just 7% of African languages are represented in AI translation tools.9 At the same time AI reached 1.2 billion users in three years, nearly 70% of them in developing countries.10 The UN Development Programme warns of a possible widening gap between countries, and says it is not inevitable.10

The ledger

What is gained

  • A shared scientific picture of the risks, backed by more than 30 countries.1
  • More developers publishing how they intend to manage risk.3
  • Rapid uptake in developing countries.10

What it costs

  • Misuse for fraud, deepfakes and cyberattacks is already documented.4
  • Computing power concentrated in a few countries and firms.8
  • Countries that rely on foreign systems gain access but little control.

What is not known

  • How capable systems will be in a few years. Experts disagree widely.
  • Whether voluntary safety frameworks hold under commercial pressure.
  • Which rules will prove effective. Evidence is only starting to come in.
