Surveillance

Cameras that recognise faces and software that predicts crime have spread to most regions. The same tools serve policing and repression.

Countries using AI surveillance75 of 176in the Carnegie index of 2019, the last global count found1
Facial recognition64 countriesin the same index; 56 with safe-city platforms, 52 with smart policing2
Spent in 11 African countries$2 bnat least, on facial recognition and vehicle tracking5

Spending on AI surveillance in three African countries

What is happening

The global picture is dated. The most cited count is from 2019: at least 75 of 176 countries used AI for surveillance, and 51% of advanced democracies did so.13 One Chinese company supplied at least fifty countries, and US firms thirty-two.2 The index records where the technology is present. It does not separate lawful from unlawful use.1 No newer global count was found.

A 2026 study of Africa. Eleven countries have spent at least $2 billion on facial recognition and number-plate systems, with Nigeria, Mauritius and Kenya the largest buyers.45 The researchers say the systems are being installed without legal frameworks strong enough to protect rights, and in some countries with little terrorism or serious crime.4

Why governments buy it. Police see clear uses in finding suspects and analysing large volumes of data, and adoption by governments was expected.6

The ledger

What is gained

  • Tools for finding suspects and missing people and for managing traffic and crowds.6

What it costs

  • Mass monitoring of public space, often without legal safeguards.4
  • Misidentification and bias in facial recognition.
  • Use against critics and minorities; the 2019 index notes that authoritarian states are more prone to abuse.3

What is not known

  • How far the technology has spread since 2019.
  • How often it prevents or solves crime. Independent evaluations are rare.
  • How newer AI models change what can be done with the footage.

F3 and this topic

Not in the model

F3 does not model this. Nothing in the model represents surveillance, privacy or civil liberties.

The figures on this page are context for readers. They do not enter any F3 run.
